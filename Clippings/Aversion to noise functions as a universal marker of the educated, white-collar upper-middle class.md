Aversion to noise functions as a universal marker of the educated, white-collar upper-middle class across the globe, not just in white western societies. 

You see the exact same decibel disparity in developing countries like India, where the baseline noise tolerance in an upscale South Delhi neighborhood is drastically lower than in a working-class area in "Jamna paar".

Cultural geography also shifts these levels independent of per capita income and education. North India operates at a higher baseline volume than the South. Punjabis project louder individually, Gujaratis function like a surround sound system when in groups, while Pahadis share the quieter disposition of South Indians and Northeasterners. 

Personally, I think that in this modern day and age the gift of quiet has never been cheaper to give other people. Earphones cost next to nothing and Bluetooth comes standard on the cheapest handset, and yet the full-volume reel in a packed metro coach survives, which suggests that for some people making noise was the point all along.

It is in fact true that one thing that seems to separate upper-middle class whites from most everyone else is a very low tolerance for noise. It's the best reason to live in an upper-middle class white neighborhood that I know of.

In my experience, noise (less than crime) is the most pressing daily problem when you live in and very near lower-income neighborhoods: It's human noise in black neighborhoods (conversations in the street and in homes and apartments are at a ridiculously high sound level), music noise in Hispanic ones, and dog noise in white ones.

Mexican music is actually painful to listen to even at low sound volumes. It's the music that the devil is going to pipe into elevators and stores in hell. But nothing is worse than the sound of dogs barking. I can't for the life of me understand how people can become desensitized to that sound.
