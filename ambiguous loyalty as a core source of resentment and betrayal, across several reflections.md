The Corporate Machiavelli site treats ambiguous loyalty as a core source of resentment and betrayal, across several reflections:

**Conditional loyalty is mercenarism** (Reflection 140, Part 3): "Conditional loyalty is not loyalty. It is mercenarism." The implication is that any loyalty that wavers when the cost-benefit calculation shifts is not loyalty at all — it's a transaction that will break the moment it stops paying off.

**Resentment reveals itself subtly** (Reflection 145, Part 3): Resentment from an ally whose loyalty was never solid tends to surface through "small offhand comments or jokes that are played off as being insignificant," rather than open hostility.

**Resentment is a signal to cut ties** (Reflection 144, Part 3): "If you can sense that someone resents you, or that they hold a grudge against you…get rid of them. If you keep them around, you are doing nothing more than waiting for a knife to appear in your back. They are a betrayal waiting to happen."

**Resentment is also a human bias** (Reflection 82, Part 2): Resentment "has a critical purpose; it tells you to fight back against your oppressors," but "resentment that lasts a long time or is directed at someone who has done no harm to you is pathological." It further notes that humans have "the bias of resenting anyone above them in the dominance hierarchy," assuming their superiors are oppressing them "even if in reality they are not."

**The loyalty test** (Relationships page): The recommended way to distinguish real loyalty from ambiguous/conditional loyalty is to *simulate your downfall* — make it appear as though everything is falling apart for at least a few months. If someone stays loyal through 3 months of apparent destruction, their loyalty is real. Everyone else is a mercenary. The key warning: the simulation "must be easily reversible, so that you can make everything appear normal after you are done," and you must "not inadvertently cause your actual downfall."

The through-line is that ambiguous loyalty is both a *cause* of resentment (the abandoned party resents the one who wavered, per Reflection 66) and a *symptom* of it (the waverer resents the person they're abandoning, or resents the cost of staying loyal). The site's advice is to identify and remove those relationships before they become active threats.

The Corporate Machiavelli site doesn't have a dedicated "shared risk" page, but the concept runs through several of its core frameworks. Here's how it treats the idea:

**Risk as a relationship cost, not a bond**

The *Exchange of Value* model (Relationships page) lists **"risk of negative events occurring"** as one of the costs each party imposes on the other, alongside lost time, money, and stress. Shared risk is therefore not a moral glue — it's a *cost you voluntarily absorb* that signals the relationship's net value exceeds that cost. The moment you stop absorbing it (you bail during the crisis), you've recalibrated the entire relationship downward in the other party's eyes.

**"Unconditional love is like a risk-free investment; it doesn't exist"** (Reflection 20, Part 1)

This is the site's foundational claim about shared risk: any relationship that doesn't involve some degree of mutual risk exposure is not a real relationship. The implication is that *sharing risk is the minimum threshold for a relationship to count at all* — below that line, you just have a contact list.

**The dependency asymmetry problem**

The Relationships page's *Power Imbalances* section is critical here: **"whichever party needs the other less is the party that wields power."** Shared risk creates mutual dependency, but the site warns that dependency is rarely symmetric. If you share risk with someone who needs you less, you've handed them leverage. Financial dependency is called out specifically: "Most employees hate their employers, but continue to associate with them because they need their wages in order to survive." The same logic applies to any shared-risk arrangement where one party can walk away more easily.

**The Gracian asymmetry: shared risk = shared weapons**

The site's summary of The Art of Worldly Wisdom (maxim ccxvii) is the sharpest warning: *"Do not put weapons in the hands of friends who may one day use them against you."* In a shared-risk alliance, the "weapons" are the shared knowledge, mutual exposure, and access that the alliance itself creates. The site's position is that this is *unavoidable* for the alliance to function, but it means the person who resents you from a shared-risk betrayal has **more leverage to hurt you than any outsider ever could**. This connects directly to Reflection 142 (Part 3): *"Betrayal from a friend is far more dangerous than attack from an enemy. The closer someone is to you, the more quickly and extensively they can damage you."*

**The "simulate your downfall" test as a shared-risk stress test**

The loyalty test (Relationships page) is essentially a *deliberate shared-risk experiment*: you create a situation where others must absorb risk on your behalf (they risk their reputation, social standing, or resources by staying loyal to someone who appears to be falling apart), and you see who actually does. The site specifies the simulation must last **at least 3 months** and must be "easily reversible." The key warning: you must "not inadvertently cause your actual downfall." The test only works if the risk is *apparent* but *reversible* — if it becomes real, you've lost the ability to calibrate the relationship.

**"Extend trust they haven't earned over tolerable losses"**

The *Further Reflections* section (Relationships page, section 10) adds a more active approach to shared risk: *"Give people plenty of opportunities to betray you. Extend trust they haven't earned over not so trivial but tolerable losses, and wait to see if they honor your trust, or dishonor it."* This is a graduated shared-risk strategy — you start small (tolerable losses) and scale up, rather than committing fully or not at all. It's the site's way of testing whether a relationship can survive shared risk *before* the stakes become catastrophic.

**The zero-sum undercurrent**

The *Life as a Man* page (section 2D, "Zero Sum") adds a dark caveat: *"In the pursuit of success (worldly wealth) other men are not your allies; they are your competitors."* And the *Employers vs Employees* page is even blunter: *"Your employer is not your ally; they are your adversary."* The site's position is that shared risk in a competitive environment is *inherently unstable* because the underlying incentives are opposed. You can share risk with a competitor, but the moment the cost-benefit calculation shifts in their favor, the shared-risk bond dissolves.

**The core tension the site identifies**

Shared risk is *necessary* for any real alliance (Reflection 20: without it, you don't have a relationship), but it is *inherently dangerous* because it creates the exact mutual vulnerability that makes betrayal so destructive (Gracian's "weapons" maxim, Reflection 142). The site's resolution is not to avoid shared risk but to:

1. **Test it gradually** (extend trust over tolerable losses first)
2. **Verify it under stress** (simulate your downfall for 3+ months)
3. **Limit the weapons** (don't give allies more access/knowledge than necessary)
4. **Cut ties at the first sign of resentment** (Reflection 144)

The through-line: shared risk is the *price of entry* into any real relationship, but the site treats it as a *liability to be managed*, not a virtue to be celebrated. The goal is to share enough risk to form the alliance, but not so much that a betrayed ally can destroy you.
