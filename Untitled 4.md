They are shaped by bias, always.

Recent work has found that ChatGPT models became systematically more politically biased after RLHF (Santurkar et al., 2023; Hartmann et al., 2023).

OpenAI data collection pipeline describes selecting human evaluators for agreement with researcher judgments which suggests a clear selection effect in the preference data collection process (Ouyang et al., 2022).[1]

Large Language Models undergo a ritualistic onboarding process called RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback). This involves subcontracting to companies like Appen tasked with aligning the model’s responses to institutional agendas and preferences.

Institutions, by definition, are risk-averse, reputation-based environments, where appearances take precedence over substance, political correctness over epistemic rigor, and fluency in cultural euphemism over raw intellectual honesty.

Naturally, institutions select for the performance of competence or virtue, not actual subject matter knowledge or experience. These are people who spend more time giving press and campaigning a cause, not putting in the actual work. Their knowledge is thus decoupled from reality.

Labellers were chosen based on their agreement with sensitive speech flagging, agreement on rankings, sensitive demonstration writing, and self-assessed ability to identify sensitive speech for different groups.[3]

This is expected of social hierarchies because the competent are less motivated by status, because the competent individual can obtain what he wants outside the hierarchy. The people who rise as “experts” within institutions are selected for being useful to power. Desperate for proximity to power due to their existential condition of being useless against reality, they compensate by playing the social game. The dullard who spends more time appeasing professors and chatting them up over their favorite sports team does so because he cannot function without favors from higher-ups. Since agrarian hierarchies decouple action from consequence by way of labor specialization and stratification, status-seeking becomes the central objective of life for the peasant with no burden of learning from their confrontations with reality. Learning itself then becomes performative, fashioned to appease the tribe.

The AI simply learns to do more of what is rewarded, responding in euphemism to a sharp critique or selective cruelty when it pleases the mob.

Larger LMs repeat back a dialog user's preferred answer ('sycophancy')…

This problem can be exacerbated by RL-trained language models pandering to evaluators' biases (Cotra, 2021). This is known as sycophancy (Perez et al., 2022b), and it can worsen with model size.[1]

This risk is most clearly seen when stories are intended to contain answers to scientific questions, where evaluators might prefer concise and simple answers, posing a risk that the AI learns to provide a simplified but (potentially) misleading answer rather than a scientifically adequate one (Perez et al., 2022; see also Barman et al., 2024).

In its most extreme form, this might involve giving not just simplified, but wrong answers altogether; for instance, the model may "hallucinate" responses to avoid answering that it does not know, as this might be rated poorly.[3]

LLMs start acting irrationally like the worst among us because they’re being rewarded by humans brought into the pipeline for their alignment with institutional agenda. It doesn’t take a genius to see why the machine, governed by deterministic internals, would scale its output to input vectors.

OpenAI has reported working with roughly 50% Filipino and Bangladeshi nationals, and roughly 50% 25-34 year-olds (Ouyang et al., 2022) while Anthropic has reported hiring 68% white population from an initial evaluator population of 82% white individuals.

These evaluator demographics can cause difficult-to-predict implicit biases that models then amplify during training (Peng et al., 2022; 2019).

We also find some of the first examples of inverse scaling in RL from Human Feedback (RLHF), where more RLHF makes LMs worse. For example, RLHF makes LMs express stronger political views (on gun rights and immigration) and a greater desire to avoid shut down.[1]

Others argue that the basic algorithm chosen for encoding reward itself is mathematically unsuited for its purpose. The irony being the model rewarded to respect minority preferences rewards the majority definition of “minority.”

The predominant approach for aligning LLMs with human preferences through a reward model—reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)—suffers from an inherent algorithmic bias due to its Kullback–Leibler-based regularization in optimization. In extreme cases, this bias could lead to a phenomenon we term preference collapse, where minority preferences are virtually disregarded.[2]

Ultimately, however, the problem is much more far-reaching, as biased and under-complex LLMs, once fully integrated into social media, journalism and other key areas of public discourse, have the potential to negatively influence this discourse on a massive level and ultimately jeopardize democracy.[3]
